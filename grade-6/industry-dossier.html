The Policy FileCan a government pick winners?

Japan spent eighty years trying. It has the most studied industrial policy in history, and economists who have read the same evidence reach opposite conclusions. Here are five cases. Your job is to reach a verdict they could not.

  • 1963The most famous story about Japanese industrial policy is about a law that never existed.
  • 2009Japan put about ¥140 billion into its national memory-chip champion. It was bankrupt 34 months later.
  • 2009–24Japan's state investment fund made about ¥1 trillion of profit across 144 investments — and one of them produced all of it.
  • 1955–90Every instrument of government support flowed towards Japan's slowest-growing industries.
  • nowJapan is spending ¥2.35 trillion on a company that has never sold a chip.

01Case file · the idea

The ministry that was said to run a country

In 1982 an American political scientist named Chalmers Johnson published MITI and the Japanese Miracle, and the argument in it changed how the world thought about economic growth. Japan, he said, was not a normal capitalist country that happened to be doing well. It was a different kind of state.

A Western government, on Johnson's account, is a regulatory state: it sets the rules of the game and referees. Japan was a developmental state: growth itself was the government's first job, and the market was a tool for producing it. He called the difference market-rational versus plan-rational.

The agency doing the planning was MITI — the Ministry of International Trade and Industry. And it held some remarkable levers.

The leverWhat it meant in practice
Foreign currency Japan had very little of it after the war, and MITI decided who got some. No currency meant no imported machines.
Technology licences Under the Foreign Capital Law, a Japanese company could not buy the rights to a foreign invention without permission. MITI granted it.
Cheap loans The Japan Development Bank lent below market rates, and where it led, private banks followed.
Administrative guidance Simply telling a company what to do. No law required obedience. Firms obeyed anyway, because MITI controlled everything in the rows above.

Chalmers Johnson, MITI and the Japanese Miracle: The Growth of Industrial Policy, 1925–1975, Stanford University Press, 1982. Full text · Publisher.

If that picture is right, then a clever, patient government really can build industries that would not otherwise exist — and every country that is now spending billions trying to do exactly that is following Japan.

Argue the other side Is the picture right? ▾

Here is the awkward thing, and almost nobody mentions it. Johnson's own book is full of MITI losing. The single longest case study in it is a bill MITI wanted and could not get — Case File 02 below. Johnson was arguing that MITI mattered structurally, not that MITI got its way.

Second, Johnson was a political scientist. His book contains almost no statistics testing whether the industries MITI helped actually did better than the ones it ignored. That work came later, and Case File 04 is what it found.

Third, a hard question about causation. Japan grew at extraordinary speed between 1950 and 1973. So did West Germany, which had nothing resembling MITI. If two countries take different routes to the same place, how much credit can the route claim?

Questions

  1. Which of the four levers in the table would be hardest for a government to use today, and why?
  2. Administrative guidance was not law, and companies obeyed it anyway. What made it work — and what does that tell you about where power actually sits?
02Case file · a myth, corrected

The law that never existed

You will hear this story. It is in business books, in talks, all over the internet. It is the single most-repeated anecdote about Japanese industrial policy — and in its usual form it is false.

The story as usually told

MITI passed a law banning new companies from making cars, to force the industry into a few big groups. Honda defied the law, built cars anyway, and went on to become one of the great car companies — proving the bureaucrats were fools.

What the record shows

There was no law. The Specified Industries Promotion Bill was put to the Diet three times in 1963–64 and died every time without a completed debate. Honda broke nothing, because there was nothing to break.

What actually happened

  • May 1961 MITI proposes sorting the car industry into three groups. Two firms for mass-market cars, two or three for luxury and sports cars, two or three for mini-vehicles. This was guidance, not law. The car makers rejected it — as they had rejected MITI's 1955 "people's car" plan.
  • Jan 1962 Honda orders crash development of two sports cars and two trucks. Honda made motorcycles. It had never built a car.
  • 22 Mar 1963 The Cabinet approves the Bill. It would have set "promotion standards" for steel, cars and petrochemicals, with tax breaks for mergers that complied, antitrust exemptions and priority state lending.
  • Aug 1963 Honda's T360 mini-truck goes on sale. Its first production automobile. The S500 sports car followed in October.
  • 1963–64 The Bill dies. Three times. Killed by a coalition: Keidanren's chairman Ishizaka Taizō called it "bureaucratic control in a different form"; the banks opposed it; the opposition parties opposed it; and MITI itself was split down the middle.

“To hell with the Specified Industry Promotion Law! I had the right to manufacture automobiles.”

Soichiro Honda, in a 1983 television interview, quoted on Honda's own corporate heritage site.

So the popular story gets the hero right and everything else wrong. Honda did race to build a production record before a proposed law could bite. But the law was then defeated by big business, the banks and parliament — not by one stubborn engineer.

Why the correction matters more than the myth

The myth says: bureaucrats are stupid, entrepreneurs are brave. The record says something far more useful — that Japan's famously all-powerful ministry proposed something important and was beaten in public by the people it was supposed to be directing. A country where that can happen is not the country in the myth. And the United States Congress's own 1991 review of Japanese industrial policy concluded that if MITI's consolidation plan had succeeded it "would by widespread agreement have damaged the industry's long-term competitiveness."

Honda corporate heritage — launching the S360 and T360 · US Office of Technology Assessment, Japanese Industrial Policy: The Postwar Record, 1991 · National Diet Library record of the Bill. Note that "no new firm could have entered" is Honda's characterisation, published on Honda's site; it is not what the Bill's text says.

Argue the other side Does the correction let MITI off? ▾

No, and it would be lazy to think so. The Bill failed, but the 1961 grouping plan was real, and MITI did not need a statute to make life hard for a new entrant — it controlled foreign currency, technology licences and the banks' lending priorities. Honda's fear was rational even though the legal threat evaporated.

There is also a sharper version of the criticism. A ministry that tried to block the future Honda, and tried in 1955 to pick a single "people's car", was wrong about the car industry twice in a decade. That it lost both times is lucky, not exculpatory.

And the strongest defence of MITI here: its officials were not idiots. In 1961 Japan was about to open to foreign competition, its car firms were tiny by world standards, and nobody knew that scale would arrive on its own. Being wrong about the future is not the same as being foolish — which is a thought worth holding when you judge Case File 05.

Questions

  1. The myth spread because it is a better story than the truth. Name one feature of the false version that makes it stickier than the real one.
  2. Honda's own website says the Bill would have kept it out of the market. The Bill's text contains no such rule. Is Honda lying, misremembering, or describing something real that the text does not capture?
  3. If MITI had won, Japan would have had two or three car companies instead of eight. Make the best case you can that this would have been good — then say why you do not believe it.
03Case file · the champion

Bankrupt in thirty-four months

By the late 1990s Japan was losing the memory-chip business it had invented. The response was textbook industrial policy: combine the survivors into one national champion big enough to fight.

WhenWhat happened
Dec 1999 NEC and Hitachi merge their DRAM businesses. Renamed Elpida in 2000.
2003Mitsubishi Electric's DRAM business is folded in too.
June 2009 The rescue. ¥30 billion of new equity from the Development Bank of Japan plus roughly ¥100 billion in loans — about ¥140 billion of support, with the government covering 80% of any losses. Elpida had just lost nearly ¥180 billion in a single quarter.
27 Feb 2012 Bankruptcy. Liabilities ¥448 billion — the largest failure of a Japanese manufacturer since the war. Elpida was the world's number three DRAM maker.
31 July 2013 Bought by Micron, of the United States. Japan has had no memory-chip maker since.

Nippon.com — Elpida and the failure of Japan Inc. · Nippon.com — the collapse and industrial policy · Micron Memory Japan. Today Samsung and SK hynix of South Korea hold 68% of world DRAM between them.

Thirty-four months from national rescue to the largest manufacturing bankruptcy in Japan's post-war history.

Argue the other side Too much policy — or not enough? ▾

The obvious reading: the government propped up a loser, delayed the reckoning by three years, and lost public money doing it. Memory chips had become a commodity — every chip identical, so the only thing that matters is building the biggest factory fastest. Japan was trying to defend cleverness in a business that had stopped rewarding cleverness.

The opposite reading, and it is held seriously. Writing in the same publication, one Japanese commentator argued the problem was too little industrial policy, not too much: ¥140 billion is small change against what Samsung was investing with South Korean state backing, and a half-hearted rescue is worse than none. On that view Japan lost because it would not commit.

Which reading you prefer determines what you think about Case File 05 — where Japan is now committing ¥2.35 trillion, about seventeen times the Elpida rescue, to a company in the same industry.

Questions

  1. What would you have needed to know in June 2009 to predict February 2012? Was that knowable at the time, or only afterwards?
  2. Japan has no memory-chip maker and is perfectly prosperous. Does a rich country actually need to make everything? Give a rule for deciding which industries a country must keep.
04Case file · the scoreboard

One win paid for a hundred and forty-three losses

From 2009 to 2024 Japan ran a state-backed investment fund, the INCJ, to build the industries it wanted. When it closed, the books looked like a triumph.

Toyo Keizai — the INCJ's fifteen years · INCJ's own summary · Dempa Shimbun on the final Japan Display sale, March 2025. Japan Display was formed in 2012 by merging the screen divisions of Sony, Toshiba and Hitachi; roughly ¥462 billion of public money went into it over thirteen years and it still has negative net assets.

Read those rows in order. The fund made a trillion yen. The fund also lost money on essentially everything except one investment. Both sentences are true.

And now the harder evidence

In 1996 two economists, Richard Beason and David Weinstein, did something nobody had bothered to do: they checked whether the industries the Japanese government supported between 1955 and 1990 actually grew faster. They looked at four instruments across thirteen sectors.

Instrument of supportCorrelation with growth
Cheap loans from the Japan Development Bank−0.31
Tax breaks−0.33
Tariffs (protection from imports)−0.31
Direct subsidies−0.13

Every single one is negative. The more help an industry got, the slower it grew. Mining was the number one recipient of cheap loans, subsidies and tax relief, and grew 3.8%. Textiles was third, and grew 2.7%. Meanwhile electrical machinery grew 12.2% while ranking eighth or ninth for support, and cars grew 10.8% while ranking between fourth and eleventh.

Richard Beason & David E. Weinstein, "Growth, Economies of Scale, and Targeting in Japan (1955–1990)", Review of Economics and Statistics 78(2), 1996. Sectoral figures restated here.

Argue the other side Does that settle it? No — and the reason is beautiful ▾

Think about what a government does. Does it hand money to the industry that is already booming — or to the one that is in trouble, laying people off, and filling the newspapers?

It helps the one in trouble. So support flows towards slow-growing industries by construction, and a negative correlation is exactly what you would see even if every yen of that help worked perfectly. This is a selection effect, and it is the same reason hospitals are full of sick people.

That is the argument made in 2024 by Réka Juhász, Nathan Lane and Dani Rodrik in the Annual Review of Economics. Their sharpest line: a government that helps losers and a government that helps winners are, in this kind of data, "observationally equivalent" — you cannot tell them apart. Newer studies using better methods, they say, are "much more favourable to industrial policy".

But do not over-correct. The same authors refuse to claim that industrial policy raises a country's overall growth rate. The defensible summary, and you may quote it: the old evidence found money flowing to slow sectors with no productivity payoff; newer methods find real effects on the shape of an economy; nobody has shown it makes a country grow faster overall.

Questions

  1. A venture capitalist expects most investments to fail and one to pay for everything. By that standard the INCJ did well. Is that the right standard for public money? Say what is different about spending taxes rather than your own savings.
  2. Explain the selection-effect problem to somebody in two sentences, without using the words "correlation" or "causation".
  3. Design a test that would tell you whether industrial policy worked. What would you need to observe? Why can nobody actually run it?

Two bets, same decade, opposite philosophies

Japan has decided it must make advanced chips again. It is trying two completely different strategies at once, and you will live to find out which was right.

Negrine, Findlay & Armstrong, "Chips in Japan", RIETI Discussion Paper 25-E-116, December 2025 · Tom's Hardware on the Rapidus roadmap, 2026 · TSMC on the Kumamoto opening. Japan allocated about ¥3.9 trillion to semiconductors in 2021–23 — 0.71% of its economy, a larger share than the United States, Germany or France spent.

“Think long and hard about skipping nodes… This can only end in tears.”

An industry expert on Rapidus's plan to jump from 40 nanometres to 2, quoted by the Center for Strategic and International Studies, 2023.

One strategy builds a champion and hopes. The other buys proven capability and accepts that the crown jewels belong to somebody else. Japan has tried the first before — that was Case File 03.

Argue the other side Is Rapidus Elpida again? ▾

The case that it is: a state-assembled champion, in the same industry, against the same competitors, attempting something its engineers have never done, with almost no private money beside it and no customer. Everything that was true of Elpida is true here, only seventeen times more expensive.

The case that it is not: Elpida was defending a commodity where the only weapon is scale. Rapidus is attacking a part of the market where Japan already owns the ground underneath — Japanese firms make roughly half the world's chip materials, over half its silicon wafers, and Tokyo Electron and Advantest sell the machines. A country that supplies the picks and shovels has a reason to think it can mine.

And the genuinely uncomfortable argument: perhaps it does not have to succeed commercially to be worth it. If chips become something countries can be cut off from, then paying ¥2.35 trillion for the ability to make them may be insurance rather than investment — the same reasoning that justifies Japan's 254 days of oil in Lesson 2. Insurance is supposed to lose money.

Questions

  1. Kumamoto is producing chips and Rapidus is not. Does that prove the Kumamoto approach is better, or are they attempting different things with different odds?
  2. If Rapidus fails in 2030, what should Japan conclude? And if it succeeds — what should it conclude then? Be careful: your two answers should not be mirror images.
  3. Japan spent a larger share of its economy on chips than the USA, Germany or France. Name one thing Japan therefore did not spend that money on.
