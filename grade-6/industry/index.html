Industry and Industrial Policy

Can a government look at the future, pick the industries a country ought to have, and make them happen? Almost every country now says yes and is spending accordingly. Japan has been running that experiment for eighty years, in public, with receipts. This lesson asks you to read the receipts.

The Cabinet Meeting

You are the Minister. One trillion yen. You decide, out loud, and then you have to make the strongest case that you have just wasted it.

Two numbers to hold in your head

Japan had 149 companies in the Fortune Global 500 in 1995. It has 40 today. Over the same period Japanese companies went from making close to half the world's semiconductors to 8.2%.

Now the complication. Over those same decades Japan also built a near-monopoly in the machines that make chips, the sensors in every phone camera, the robots in everybody else's factories, and about four out of every ten zippers on Earth. Both of those things happened. Any story you tell has to account for both.

The warning that applies to this whole lesson. Several of the famous stories about Japanese industrial policy are wrong — not exaggerated, wrong. Case File 02 is one of them, and you will find the real version more interesting than the myth. Do not repeat a good story just because it is a good story.

What you are being asked to do

Reach a verdict on a question that professional economists have argued about for forty years and still disagree on. You will not settle it. You are expected to commit to an answer anyway, support it with numbers that carry a year and a source, and then write the strongest possible case against yourself.
