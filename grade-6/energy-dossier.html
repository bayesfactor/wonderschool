The Energy FileFive problems with no clean answer

Japan has almost no coal worth digging, no oil, and no gas. It has 123 million people, the world's fourth-largest economy, and a hundred and fifty years of having to solve the same problem over and over. Here is what it has tried.

  • 1941Japan imported about 85% of its oil from the United States — and then attacked it.
  • 2011Japan switched off every one of its 54 nuclear reactors after an accident that killed, by radiation, one person.
  • 2023Japan committed over ¥15 trillion to hydrogen — while the number of hydrogen filling stations in Japan was falling.
  • 2022Japan sanctioned Russia and kept its stake in a Russian gas field.
  • nowTwo careful institutions report Japan's coal share as 28% and 32% in the same year. Both are right.

01Case file · the first energy war

A country that went to war for oil

By 1941 Japan had been fighting in China for four years and had almost no oil of its own. Roughly 85% of the oil it used came from the United States — the country it was increasingly at odds with. This is the single strangest fact in twentieth-century strategy: Japan's war machine ran on American fuel.

In July 1941 Japanese forces moved into southern French Indochina. On 26 July 1941 President Roosevelt froze all Japanese assets in the United States and placed oil exports under licence — a licence that was never granted. The tap closed.

Japan's navy calculated it had roughly eighteen months of fuel. Its army, about twelve. After that, the fleet would stop moving — not be defeated, simply stop.

There were two options. Withdraw from China and get the oil back. Or seize the oilfields of the Dutch East Indies — today's Indonesia — which meant taking the Philippines on the way, which meant war with the United States. On 7 December 1941 Japan chose the second.

What the oil actually did

  • Jan–Feb 1942 Japan captures the oilfields. Tarakan and Balikpapan in Borneo, then Palembang in Sumatra, taken by paratroopers. The plan worked.
  • 1943 The oil is in the wrong place. Crude in Sumatra is no use in Osaka. It has to travel four thousand miles by tanker, through water the United States Navy is learning to control.
  • 1944 American submarines sink the tankers. Not the warships — the tankers. Japan had captured the oil and lost the ability to carry it.
  • 1945 Japan tries to make fuel from pine roots. Schoolchildren were sent to dig up pine stumps to be distilled into aviation fuel. It never worked at anything like the scale needed.
Argue the other side Was it really about oil? ▾

A historian could reasonably say: no. Japan had been expanding into Asia since 1895, long before oil mattered. The ideology of empire, the army's political power, and the humiliation of the 1920s naval treaties were all pushing the same direction. Oil was the occasion, not the cause — the embargo forced a timetable onto a decision that had already been made.

There is a second objection, and it is sharper. If oil was the reason, the decision was catastrophically bad at its own game: Japan attacked the world's largest oil producer in order to secure oil. A country genuinely optimising for fuel supply would have withdrawn from China. So either oil was not the real goal, or the decision-makers were not reasoning about oil very well.

Hold both. The embargo is why it happened in December 1941. It is not, by itself, why it happened at all.

Questions

  1. Japan captured the oilfields and still ran out of fuel. What does that tell you about the difference between having a resource and having access to one?
  2. The United States cut off oil to stop a war and started a bigger one. When is an embargo a good idea? Give a condition, not an opinion.
  3. Japan today holds about 254 days of oil in storage. In 1941 it held roughly 18 months for the navy. Which country was better prepared, and what does your answer depend on?
02Case file · the switch-off

The day 54 reactors stopped

On 11 March 2011 a magnitude 9.0 earthquake struck off north-east Japan. The reactors at Fukushima Daiichi shut down automatically, as designed. Then the tsunami arrived, flooded the backup generators, and the cooling stopped. Three cores melted.

What followed was not an engineering decision but a political one. Reactor by reactor, as each came up for routine maintenance, local governors refused permission to restart. On 5 May 2012 the last one — Tomari 3 — shut down, and for the first time since 1970 Japan made no nuclear power at all.

A country that got roughly a quarter of its electricity from nuclear power replaced all of it, in about a year, mostly by burning imported gas and coal.

What it cost

Energy self-sufficiency, by Japanese fiscal year. Nuclear counts as domestic under the Japanese method, which is why switching the reactors off moves this number so violently. OpenGov Japan, from government statistics.

Japan's electricity bills rose. Its trade balance flipped from surplus to deficit as it bought gas and coal on world markets. Its carbon emissions rose for three years, then began falling again as efficiency measures and solar took hold.

Read those three rows again. The evacuation ordered to protect people from radiation appears to have killed roughly two thousand times as many people as the radiation did.

Argue the other side So the shutdown was irrational? ▾

Careful. Three separate arguments are hiding in that sentence and they do not all survive.

The strong version of the case against the shutdown: the reactors that were switched off were not the ones that failed. Burning coal and gas instead has costs too — air pollution from fossil generation kills people every year, quietly, and nobody evacuates a city for it. If you count deaths per unit of electricity, nuclear is among the safest things humans have ever built.

The strong version of the case for it: Japan's regulator had been captured by the industry it supervised. Fukushima Daiichi's sea wall was known to be too low. Turning everything off and rebuilding the regulator from scratch — which is what the NRA was, in 2012 — was a reasonable response to discovering that the safety system on paper was not the safety system in reality. And the evacuation deaths are an argument against how the evacuation was done, not against caution itself.

And a harder one: a democracy is allowed to decide it does not want something, even if the expected-value calculation says otherwise. The people who had to leave their homes were not asked to run the numbers. Whether that is wisdom or weakness is a real question, and neither answer is obviously stupid.

Questions

  1. Fifteen reactors have restarted, and Kashiwazaki-Kariwa 6 came back in February 2026 — the first reactor its owner had run since the accident. What changed between 2012 and 2026: the technology, the evidence, or the politics?
  2. Suppose you could prove that the extra coal burned between 2011 and 2015 shortened more lives than the accident did. Should that settle the argument? Say what your answer assumes.
  3. Japan's self-sufficiency fell from 20.2% to 6.3%. Name one thing that becomes possible for a rival country when a country's number falls that far.
03Case file · the hydrogen bet

Why Japan is so keen on fuel cells

No other country has gone as far into hydrogen as Japan. It published the world's first national hydrogen strategy in December 2017, revised it in June 2023 with over ¥15 trillion of planned public and private investment, and has installed more home fuel cells than the rest of the world combined. The question worth asking is why, because the usual answer — climate — is not the main one.

Four reasons, in order of how much they actually explain

ReasonWhat it means
Security Hydrogen can be made from almost anything, almost anywhere — gas, coal, sunlight, spare nuclear power. A country that must import its energy would much rather import something with many possible sellers than oil, which has few.
Industry Toyota, Honda, Panasonic, Toshiba and Kawasaki hold a great many hydrogen patents. A hydrogen world is a world Japanese firms would sell into. This is industrial policy as much as energy policy, and Japan does not much hide it.
Efficiency A fuel cell makes electricity without burning or spinning anything. The best Japanese home unit reaches 55% electrical efficiency, and 87% once the waste heat is used for the bath — better than a large power station, in a box beside the house.
The awkward jobs Steel, shipping, long-haul lorries and chemicals are all hard to run on batteries. Something dense and storable has to do those, and hydrogen or ammonia is the leading candidate.

Efficiency figures: ENE-FARM Type S, 2020 model. Daigas Group. Strategy figures: June 2023 revision, summarised by White & Case — targets of 3 million tonnes a year by 2030, 12 million by 2040 and 20 million by 2050.

Add a fifth reason that nobody writes down. After 2011, millions of Japanese households discovered that the grid can fail. A machine in your own basement that makes electricity and hot water from the gas pipe is, among other things, a promise that the next disaster will be less cold.

Argue the other side Is the hydrogen strategy a mistake? ▾

The case that it is: every time you convert energy you lose some. Electricity → hydrogen → back to electricity loses roughly two-thirds of what you started with. A battery loses about a tenth. If the goal is to move energy around cleanly, hydrogen is the expensive way to do it, and the FCV numbers suggest the market has already decided. Critics argue the strategy survives because it lets Japan keep burning things — ammonia in coal plants, gas reformed into hydrogen — while sounding like a transition.

The case that it is not: round-trip efficiency is the wrong test for the jobs hydrogen is actually for. Nobody proposes turning hydrogen back into grid electricity as the main use. They propose making steel with it, fuelling ships with it, and storing energy for months rather than hours — none of which a battery does. On those jobs, hydrogen's competition is not batteries; it is coal.

And the honest middle: Japan may be right about the destination and wrong about the vehicle. Home fuel cells worked. Hydrogen cars did not. A strategy that counts both as “hydrogen” hides the difference — and the difference is the whole lesson.

Questions

  1. Home fuel cells sold half a million units; hydrogen cars sold almost none. Both are the same technology. What is different about the two situations?
  2. Japan's hydrogen is mostly made from imported natural gas. In what sense does that improve Japan's energy security, and in what sense does it not?
  3. If you were told to spend ¥15 trillion to make Japan more energy-secure, and you could not spend it on hydrogen, what would you spend it on? Defend the choice against the objection that it will not work either.
04Case file · wars now

Two wars, one island

Japan's energy problem is not history. It is happening this decade, in two places at once, and the two force opposite decisions.

Russia: the gas you sanction and keep buying

When Russia invaded Ukraine in February 2022, Japan joined the G7 sanctions. It also kept its stake in Sakhalin-2, a gas project on a Russian island a few hundred miles north of Hokkaido. Mitsui holds 12.5%; Mitsubishi holds 10%. Russia supplied about 9% of Japan's LNG in 2024.

Why keep it? Because Sakhalin gas reaches Japan in two or three days. Australian gas takes about ten; Qatari gas takes three weeks and passes through the Strait of Hormuz. Giving up Sakhalin would not have reduced Japan's emissions or Russia's income by much — it would have handed the cargoes to China and made Japan's supply longer and more fragile.

Japan spent a decade successfully reducing its Middle Eastern gas dependence from 29% to 11%. Over the same decade its Middle Eastern oil dependence did not fall at all. Ask why the two went differently.

Argue the other side Is 254 days of oil enough? ▾

Yes: the IEA asks its members to hold 90 days. Japan holds nearly three times that. No closure of Hormuz in history has lasted anything close to eight months, because the countries that would be strangled by it include the ones doing the strangling. Stockpiles are insurance against a shock, and eight months is a lot of insurance.

No: a stockpile buys time; it does not buy a solution. If the strait closed on day one, what exactly is Japan doing on day 253? The stock only helps if the interruption is temporary and Japan uses the time to build something. And oil is not the only thing on those ships — fertiliser, plastics and chemicals come the same way.

The sharper objection: holding 254 days may actually reduce the pressure to fix the underlying dependence. Insurance that is too good makes you careless. That argument is uncomfortable, and it is not obviously wrong.

Questions

  1. Japan sanctioned Russia and kept the gas field. Is that hypocrisy, or is it what any country would do? Give a rule that distinguishes the two.
  2. Japan cut Middle Eastern gas from 29% to 11% but could not cut Middle Eastern oil at all. What is different about oil that makes it harder to move away from?
  3. Which single change would do most for Japan's energy security: more stockpile, more reactors, more renewables, or more suppliers? Pick one and say what you are giving up.
05Case file · the measurement problem

The chart that disagrees with itself

In lesson 1 you found that nobody has measured Japan's literacy rate since 1948, and that the famous 99% is a gap somebody filled in. Here is the same disease in a different organ.

Two respected institutions published Japan's 2025 electricity mix. Neither is careless. Neither is lying. They do not agree.

Source in the mix ISEP (Tokyo) Ember (London) Gap
Gas / LNG28.9%33%4.1
Coal28.4%32%3.6
Nuclear9.4%9.1%0.3
Solar9.9%9.8%0.1
Wind1.4%1.3%0.1
All fossil64.5%67%2.5

ISEP: Institute for Sustainable Energy Policies, 2025 preliminary, published September 2026. Ember: Global Electricity Review 2026.

Notice which rows disagree. Nuclear, solar and wind match almost exactly. Coal and gas are out by three to four points. That pattern is the clue.

The answer

The two organisations are dividing by different denominators. ISEP counts all electricity generated in Japan, including power that factories and steel mills make for themselves and never sell. Ember counts the public grid. Factories mostly self-generate with gas and coal — so adding them to the bottom of the fraction dilutes coal and gas, and leaves nuclear, solar and wind almost untouched.

Both numbers are correct answers to slightly different questions. The mistake is not choosing one. The mistake is quoting ISEP's solar share next to Ember's coal share, which produces a chart of a country that does not exist.

Argue the other side Then which one should a school use? ▾

It depends what you are asking. “How clean is the electricity in my plug?” is a grid question — use Ember. “How much carbon does Japanese electricity generation emit in total?” is a whole-country question — use ISEP. If you are comparing Japan with Germany, use whichever source measured both the same way, which in practice means Ember, because it applies one method everywhere.

The genuinely wrong answer is “whichever number supports what I already wanted to say.” You can spot people doing this: they will quote the 32% coal figure when criticising Japan and the 26.7% renewables figure when praising it, and those two come from different publications.

Questions

  1. Without looking it up: if a country's steel industry collapsed, which way would ISEP's coal share move, and why?
  2. ISEP's own report says Japan's solar share for 2024 could be anywhere from 9.6% to 11.4% depending on method. They published 11.4%. Is that dishonest? What would you have published?
  3. Find one number in the Data Desk that you could not check from the linked source in under two minutes. What does that tell you about the number?
