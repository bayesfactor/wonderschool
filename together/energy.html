The Energy Meeting

Japan can build ten new things. That is the whole budget. Four people at the table, each holding one thing nobody else can do. Nothing gets decided unless all four agree — and the youngest person at the table has the first veto.

Nothing to print. Read your role off the screen, or have the teacher read them out. You will need one sheet of plain paper for the wall chart and a device for the Control Room.

The four roles

Grades 2–3
The Islander

You are the only person who knows what Japan actually has. Everyone else keeps forgetting, and it is your job to keep saying it.

  • Draw Japan in the middle of the paper: four islands, mountains down the spine.
  • Write on it, big: 16 out of 100.
  • Draw the sun, a river, a volcano, and the wind. Those are the things Japan has.
  • Draw a ship arriving. That is everything else.

Your powerWhenever anyone proposes something, you get to ask: “does Japan have that?” If the answer is no, they must say where it comes from before the table may vote.

Grades 3–4
The Engineer

You run the Control Room on a device. You are the only one who can tell the table whether their plan actually keeps the lights on.

  • Put the table's ten choices into the Control Room and read out the three dials.
  • Draw the tsunami card. Then the dark-week card. Report what happens.
  • Say the numbers out loud. Do not soften them.

Your powerNo plan is agreed until you have tested it and said the words “the lights stayed on”. If they did not, the table must change something.

Grades 4–5
The Quartermaster

You hold the Power Source Cards and you own the cost column. Nothing reaches the wall chart without a price on it.

  • Write the cost score and the carbon number next to every item the table picks.
  • Find one choice that is cheap now and expensive later. Say so.
  • Keep a running total. Announce it every time it changes.

Your powerYou may veto one item on cost alone, once, at any point. You must explain the veto in one sentence — and you may not use it in the last five minutes.

Grades 5–6
The Minister

You write the decision at the bottom of the chart and read it out. Three sentences, and the third one has to hurt.

  • Sentence 1: what Japan is building, and the ten items.
  • Sentence 2: the one number that justifies it.
  • Sentence 3: the strongest argument that you have got this wrong.
  • You may only use numbers the Quartermaster wrote down.

Your powerNone at all. You cannot build anything, price anything, or test anything. You can only persuade the other three — and then take the blame. That is the job.

The shared budget board

Ten things, and the table has to agree on all ten. Add and remove until everybody can live with it — then send it to the Engineer.

How the forty minutes go

  1. The Islander draws first, alone, for five minutes. Nobody else touches the paper. The map and the words 16 out of 100 have to be there before anything else.
  2. Everyone proposes. Go round the table youngest first. Each person names one thing Japan should build and says why in one sentence. The Islander may challenge any of them.
  3. Fill the board to ten. Argue. The Quartermaster prices everything; the Islander asks where it comes from.
  4. The Engineer tests it. Ten choices go into the Control Room. Read the dials out loud. Draw the tsunami card and the dark-week card.
  5. Rebuild once. You are allowed exactly one rebuild after the test. Choose what to change.
  6. The Minister writes the three sentences and reads them to the table.
  7. The challenge round, last five minutes. See below.

The challenge round

The teacher asks; anybody may answer. A correct answer from the youngest student is worth three points, from the oldest, one. Read these out in any order.

3 pointsHow many out of a hundred does Japan make for itself? (About 16.)
3 pointsWhat does 火力 mean, literally? (Fire power.)
2 pointsName one thing that works at night and one thing that does not.
2 pointsWhy can't we just save electricity in the wire until we need it?
2 pointsIn 2011 Japan switched off all 54 of something. All 54 of what?
1 pointWhat fraction of Japan's oil comes through the Strait of Hormuz? (About 93%.)
1 pointTwo organisations say Japan's coal share is 28% and 32%. Which is lying? (Neither. Different denominators.)
3 pointsThe one worth most: name something the table decided that you personally think is wrong, and say why. Anyone may answer. The Minister may not.
