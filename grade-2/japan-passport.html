1 Island Hunt

How many islands is Japan?

Japan is not one piece of land. It is a chain of islands in the Pacific Ocean. Move the slider to make your guess, then press the button.

7,000

Four islands are much bigger than all the rest. Almost everybody lives on these four: Honshu (the biggest — Tokyo is here), Hokkaido (the snowy one at the top), Kyushu (the one at the bottom) and Shikoku (the smallest).

✎

On your paper: draw Japan. Four big islands in a curve, like a banana, with lots of tiny dots all around. Label the four big ones.

2 The Mountain That Is a Volcano

Mount Fuji is 12,389 feet tall

That is more than two miles straight up. And it is not just a mountain — it is a volcano, and it is still awake. It last erupted in 1707.

Tap a bar to see how tall it is.

Do the maths: this year is 2026. Mount Fuji erupted in 1707. How many years ago was that? Work it out before you scroll on.

A Japanese artist called Hokusai loved this mountain so much that he drew it thirty-six different times — in storms, in snow, behind a giant wave, near and far.

✎

On your paper: be Hokusai. Draw the same mountain three times, and change the season each time. What is different in winter? In spring?

3 Words That Are Pictures

Tap a card to find the picture

English has 26 letters. Japan uses three writing systems at the same time: hiragana, katakana and kanji. Kanji began as tiny pictures of real things. Can you spot the picture hiding in each one?

Whoa: a second grader in Japan learns 160 brand new kanji this year, on top of the 80 they learned last year. By the end of sixth grade they know 1,026.

✎

On your paper — the hard one: invent your own kanji for rain, cat and happy. Then explain to a grown-up why you drew it that way. Explaining it is the tricky part.

Six of these kanji are printed on your Explorer Sheet so you can trace them.

4 Games, Sports and Surprises

Japan made things you already love

The most popular sport in Japan is baseball — about 45 people out of every 100 follow it. Second is sumo. Third is soccer.

  • 1889 Nintendo is founded. For its first hundred years it made paper playing cards. No video games at all.
  • 1958 Instant ramen noodles. Invented by Momofuku Ando in a shed in his back garden.
  • 1964 The bullet train. The Shinkansen ran at 130 mph when other trains managed 80.
  • 1971 Karaoke. Daisuke Inoue never patented it and made almost no money from it.
  • 1994 The QR code. Masahiro Hara let everybody use it for free. That is why it is everywhere.
  • 1996 Pokémon. Satoshi Tajiri got the idea from collecting bugs as a boy.
✎

On your paper: invent a brand new game character. Not one you have seen before — a new one, out of your own brain. Draw it, name it, and write one sentence about its special power.

5 The Red Gate

Walking into a different world

That is a torii (say it: TOR-ee-ee). It marks the front door of a shrine. Walk through it and you are stepping out of the everyday world and into a special one.

Japan has thousands of them. Some stand in deep forests. One famous one stands out in the sea, and at high tide it looks like it is floating.

Here is the surprising part. If you ask people in Japan "what is your religion?", about 7 out of 10 say they do not have one. But almost everyone still visits a shrine at New Year to wish for a good year.

So — can you do a religious thing without being religious? There is no wrong answer. Second graders often have better answers to this than grown-ups.

✎

On your paper: draw a torii gate. Two tall posts, two beams across the top, and the top one curves up at the ends like a smile. Then put your gate somewhere — a forest, a mountain, the ocean. Colour it red-orange.

6 The Final Challenge

Write a haiku

A haiku is a very short Japanese poem. Three lines. 5 beats, then 7 beats, then 5 beats. Clap as you say each word: Ja-pan is 2 claps, vol-ca-no is 3.

+ Bonus

Fold a paper cup

This is real origami. When you finish, it actually holds water. Test it at the sink. Start with a square piece of paper.

  1. Fold the square in half into a triangle. Point it down like an ice cream cone.
  2. Take the bottom right corner. Fold it up and across to touch the middle of the left side. Press hard.
  3. Take the bottom left corner. Fold it up to touch the middle of the right side. It should look like an envelope now.
  4. Two loose flaps sit at the top. Fold the front one down toward you.
  5. Flip it over. Fold the other flap down too.
  6. Gently pull the two sides apart. It opens into a cup.

If it leaks, your folds were not tight enough. Try again — that is exactly what engineers do.
