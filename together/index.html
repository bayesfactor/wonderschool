Grades 2–6 · 2 to 4 students · about 25 minutes

The Japan Desk

One wall chart about Japan. Four jobs. Nobody can finish their job without somebody else's — which is the point. The second grader is not helping the sixth grader; the sixth grader cannot write a word until the second grader has drawn the space for it.

You need: one large sheet of paper (tape two together if you have none), pencils and colored pencils, the printed Country Cards, and the role cards — one page, cut into four, one each.

Warm-up: the island guess

Everybody writes a number on a scrap of paper — how many islands is Japan? — then all reveal at once. Closest wins. Nobody says their guess out loud first, because the youngest will copy the oldest and then nobody learns anything. The answer is 14,125, recounted in 2023, up from the 6,852 Japan had been claiming for thirty-five years. Ask the winner why a country loses track of seven thousand islands.

The four jobs

Grades 2–3
The Illustrator

You own everything anyone can see. You decide the layout, which means you decide how much room each person gets.

  • Draw Japan: four big islands in a curve, tiny dots all around.
  • Draw a torii gate and Mount Fuji somewhere on the chart.
  • Write headings in kanji where you know one — 山 mountain, 川 river, 日 sun, 人 person.
  • Write a haiku across the top.

Your powerYou rule the layout. If someone wants more space they have to ask you, and you can say no.

Grades 3–4
The Quartermaster

You run the Country Cards deck and supply every number that goes on the chart. No number reaches the wall except through you.

  • Deal the cards and find three categories where Japan beats the USA.
  • Find three where Japan loses.
  • Hand each number to the Fact-checker on a slip with the category written on it.

Your powerThe Analyst cannot write the verdict until you have delivered at least four approved numbers. They have to wait for you.

Grades 4–5
The Fact-checker

Nothing goes on the chart without a source written next to it. Not the drawings — the claims.

  • For every number, write where it came from and what year it is from.
  • If nobody can say where a number came from, it does not go up. Cross it out.
  • Catch at least one claim that somebody assumed rather than checked.

Your powerA veto over everyone, including the oldest person at the table. Use it at least twice or you are not doing the job.

Grades 5–6
The Analyst

You write the verdict at the bottom of the chart. Two sentences, and a third that argues against you.

  • Sentence 1: is Japan a rich country? Commit — no "it depends".
  • Sentence 2: is Japan a free country? Same rule.
  • Sentence 3: the strongest argument that you are wrong.

Your powerNone. You have to persuade the Illustrator for space and wait for the Quartermaster's numbers. That is the job.

Running it

  1. Assign jobs by age, oldest to the Analyst. If two students are close in age, let them pick — but nobody does the job they did last time.
  2. Illustrator draws first, alone, for five minutes. Everyone else reads their own material and gets ready. The chart has to exist before anyone can argue about it.
  3. Quartermaster and Fact-checker work as a pair. Numbers get found, sourced, and either approved or killed. Expect arguments. Arguments are the lesson.
  4. Analyst writes last, using only approved numbers. If the Quartermaster was slow, the Analyst writes a shorter verdict. Do not rescue them.
  5. Challenge round. Rules below.

Challenge round

The teacher asks questions about Japan and anybody may answer. A correct answer from the youngest student is worth three points; from the oldest, one point. Announce this rule before the group work starts, not after — it makes the older students teach the younger ones during the activity, because that is how their team wins.

Ten questions that work at every age: How many islands? Name two of the four big ones. What is a torii? What is the most popular sport? How many sumo tournaments a year? What year was Nintendo founded? Who invented Pokémon? What is Japan's rank by total economy? What is its rank by money per person? Why are those two different?

If you have fewer than four students

StudentsHow to split the jobs
ThreeIllustrator · Quartermaster · Analyst. The teacher plays Fact-checker and is deliberately annoying about sources.
TwoYounger takes Illustrator + Quartermaster. Older takes Fact-checker + Analyst — which means they must fact-check their own evidence before using it, which is harder and fairer.

What success looks like: at least one number got crossed off the chart for having no source, at least one older student explained something to a younger one without being asked, and the verdict at the bottom contains a real argument against itself. A beautiful chart with unsourced numbers on it is a failed chart.
